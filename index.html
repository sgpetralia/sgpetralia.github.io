Sergio Gabriel Petralia

Assistant Professor (tenured) • Utrecht University

I am an Assistant Professor (tenured) at Utrecht University. Previously, I was an ERC Postdoctoral Researcher and Visiting Researcher at the London School of Economics (LSE) and an Affiliated Associate at the Center for International Development (Growth Lab) at Harvard University. I completed my Ph.D. in Economics at Utrecht University in 2017.

Across my work, I study how technologies develop, become useful, and reshape economic activity: from the early emergence of breakthrough inventions and general-purpose technologies, to how software and AI turn knowledge into practical tools, and how these technologies spread across firms, teams, and places. To study these dynamics, I build large-scale datasets linking full-text patent records, open-source code repositories, and the tasks involved in scientific research.

Work in Progress

Actionable Disclosure in Patents: How Open Code Reduces Spatial Barriers to Knowledge Diffusion

Petralia, S., & Boschma, R.
Working Paper
SSRN Paper ↗
The patent system rests on a fundamental bargain: temporary monopoly rights in exchange for the disclosure of technical knowledge. Yet, the complexity of transferring technical know-how often limits the effectiveness of disclosure, keeping innovation highly localized. We study whether actionable disclosure in the form of publicly available executable code can overcome these frictions. We rely on a novel dataset linking two decades of patenting activity in the United States to contributions in public code repositories by 1,556 organizations. Using neural language models, we measure the semantic similarity between patent descriptions and public code contributions by these firms to identify patents with high digital disclosure. We find that these patents attract citations from inventors located approximately 17% farther away than those citing a group of control patents, suggesting that actionable disclosure in the form of executable code reduces spatial barriers to knowledge diffusion.

A new method for the early detection of important inventions

Kemeny, T., Petralia, S., & Storper, M.
Revise and resubmit at Research Policy
SSRN Paper ↗
We develop a method for early detection of important inventions by tracking how quickly technical concepts diffuse across patent texts. Using natural language processing on approximately 10 million patents granted by the USPTO (1920–2024), we show that such inventions can be identified by the presence of fast-diffusing concepts. Our method produces predictions closely aligned with frontier measures of patent novelty, significance, breakthroughs, and economic value—including the rare, groundbreaking inventions that define a technology’s trajectory—but does so significantly earlier than existing methods allow.

CLEARPAT: AI-curated full-text USPTO patent data, 1790–1975

Petralia, S., Kemeny, T., Storper, M., Hellinga, Z., & Vroom, M.
Revise and resubmit at Nature Scientific Data
The study of long-run technological change depends on access to full patent texts, yet none exist for United States patents prior to 1976. Researchers have instead relied on bibliometric proxies such as citation counts and technology class assignments, which capture little of the technical substance recorded in patent specifications. We present CLEARPAT, a dataset of high-fidelity full texts for 3.9 million U.S. utility patents granted between 1836 and 1975, alongside title, date, and patentee records for 9,462 patents granted from 1790. Full texts are produced by a dual-OCR pipeline in which two vision-language engines process digitized patent images and a large language model adjudicates conflicts between their outputs, substantially reducing character error rates relative to single-engine OCR. We extract and validate all key fields, including claims, descriptions, inventor details, geocoded locations, and citations. CLEARPAT appends to born-digital USPTO records from 1976 onward, yielding a continuous record of American patenting from 1790 to the present, with full text from 1836, and supporting research on technological change, knowledge diffusion, and science-technology relationships.
